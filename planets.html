Why do planets go vakra?

Every planet moves steadily forward around the Sun. Seen from the Earth, some of them seem to stop and walk backward for weeks. Press the button and move your viewpoint from the Sun to the Earth to see why.

The numbers behind the picture

The periods are the ones printed in Basics of Panchangam Achara Vichara (pages 4–5). Orbit sizes follow from them by Kepler's third law: the square of the period grows like the cube of the distance.

Why the loops happen

An inner planet (Budha, Shukra) runs faster than the Earth. When it passes between us and the Sun, it overtakes us and looks to be moving backward. An outer planet (Kuja, Guru, Shani) runs slower. When the Earth overtakes it, it falls behind against the stars, like a slower car seen from a faster one. The time between two vakras is the time for one of them to lap the other: 1 ÷ (1/Tfaster − 1/Tslower).

What this model leaves out

  • Orbits are drawn as circles in one plane. The booklet notes they are ovals; for Kuja, the most oval of these, a vakra can start up to about a month off. Shani and Guru stay within a few days.
  • Rahu and Ketu are shown at their average position, moving backward at the booklet's rate of one turn in 6,793 days.
  • The booklet gives the Moon's turn as 27.32966 days. I believe this is a misprint for 27.32166 days: that value gives the familiar 29.5306-day lunar month, while 27.32966 gives 29.5399 days. The Moon is not shown on this page.
